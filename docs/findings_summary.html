Most ventilated patients are not on fentanyl

At equal illness severity, fentanyl intensity tracks age and sex

Table 1 is now stratified on predominant fentanyl intensity — over an episode's at-risk time the patient is in exactly one declared band at all times, and the band holding the most time labels the episode. A declared rule, not a latent one, and an ordered one, so the p-value is a trend test rather than an omnibus one.

The gradient is steep, and it is not a severity gradient. SOFA is flat across all four strata (8, 8, 8, 7; p = 0.40) and so is the vasopressor requirement (p = 0.29). What moves is who the patient is: the high-intensity stratum is 14 years younger than the zero stratum and nine points more male, and it receives twice the propofol alongside the fentanyl.

Two artifacts reported, not argued away

This is not a trajectory: modal time ignores order, and only 41.5% of episodes end in the band they began in, so "started high and weaned" and "stayed medium throughout" can share a label. Table 1 therefore carries two extra rows — modal share, how decisive each label is (median 0.8, but 0.6 in the medium band), and at-risk hours, which expose a duration confound: a short course cannot accumulate zero windows, so the low band skews short (32h vs 68h).

03

Titration practice

Pharmacologically, turning an infusion up reaches the new steady state over four to five half-lives; a bolus given at the same moment gets there in minutes. So: when the rate goes up, does a bolus go with it?

Rate decreases are carried as a negative control. A bolus at a downtitration has no pharmacologic rationale, so its pairing rate is the floor for coincidence — the increase figures should be read against it, not against zero.

The unit of analysis is the event, not the patient. One episode contributes several rate changes, so the percentages below are the share of rate-change events paired with a bolus. The encounter-level view is in the paragraph beneath, and the two answer different questions.

Uptitrations pair 4.4× more often than downtitrations — and the ratio is largest at the narrowest window: 9.1× at ±5 min, 4.4× at ±30, 3.1× at ±60. Coincidence accumulates as the window widens, so a gap already present at five minutes is real practice rather than background bolus frequency.

But the practice is uncommon, and this is where the encounter-level view matters. Of the 7,407 episodes contributing at least one rate increase, median per-encounter adherence is zero, and among the 5,390 with two or more increases 69.7% never once pair a bolus with one (75.8% within the first 24 hours). Whether that is a quality gap or a reasonable clinical choice is a question for the group, not for the data.

One caveat to carry into any inferential follow-up: events are clustered within patients (3.25 increases per episode), so an event-level percentage has no valid confidence interval without accounting for that clustering. The descriptive figures above are presented as counts for exactly that reason.

04

A correction worth knowing about

While preparing the titration work we found that the hourly exposure grid took the maximum rate charted in each hour rather than the last, which is what the protocol specified. The two agree on a single record and on an uptitration, and diverge wherever the rate falls inside an hour — so a charted stop was invisible whenever anything else was charted in the same hour.

The error was always upward: 9,312 hourly cells affected (4.0%), median 50 mcg/hr, touching 35.0% of episodes at least once. Fixed, pinned with nine regression tests, and every shareable number re-derived and diffed.

The practical consequence is modest but real, and it moved numbers we had already looked at: mean fentanyl at hour 0 went from 47.1 to 44.5 mcg/hr, and the declared intensity bands from 50.8/21.3/12.5/15.5% to 51.6/21.4/12.2/14.8%. Cohort construction was untouched — the STROBE flow, retention and missingness tables are byte-identical before and after — so this is a change to exposure measurement only. Any figure circulated before this point should be discarded.

05

Open for the group

Five decisions we have deliberately not made alone. The first three change what the paper claims; the last two change its scope.

Are compassionate extubations in our cohort?

Of 7,026 episodes extubated within 72 hours, 1,081 (15.4%) did not survive the hospitalisation. Escalating fentanyl into a terminal extubation is a different process from analgesia during ventilation, and our continuous → extubated transition currently pools the two. This is a change to the study population, not a figure tweak.

Do the dose-intensity figures stay?

A second state definition cuts the same windows into declared intensity bands (0 / ≤200 / ≤400 / >400 mcg per 4h window). It triangulates the route-based story but is not among the five planned figures. Main text, supplement, or cut.

Does the exemplar read?

F1 shows one real de-identified patient, drawn at random from the 224 episodes meeting pre-specified criteria — never chosen by inspection. If it reads badly the fix is to change the rule and re-draw, not to pick a different patient.

Do we pursue titration outcomes?

The obvious next question is whether pairing boluses with uptitration is associated with lower cumulative dose or faster liberation. Two traps make that harder than it looks: adherence and cumulative dose are partly tautological when measured over the same window, and an episode-long exposure against time-to-extubation is immortal-time bias. Per-encounter adherence is already emitted within a 24-hour landmark so a defensible analysis is possible — but it is a second paper's worth of work.
